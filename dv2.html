Australia is Heating Up

A century of climate data reveals an unmistakable truth: Australia's temperatures are rising faster than ever, with record-breaking heat becoming the new normal across every corner of the continent.

+1.7°C Warming since 1910

Section 01 — The Big Picture

A Century of Rising Heat

Since records began in 1910, Australia's average temperature has risen by approximately 1.7°C. The warming is not gradual — it has accelerated sharply since the 1980s, with the most recent decade being the hottest on record.

Key Finding

Australia's 10 hottest years on record have all occurred since 2005. The year 2019 was the hottest ever recorded, coinciding with the catastrophic Black Summer bushfire season.

Annual Temperature Anomaly, 1910–2024

Deviation from the 1961–1990 baseline average. Red bars indicate years warmer than average; blue bars indicate cooler-than-average years. Every year since 1997 has been above the baseline.

💡 The shift from predominantly blue (cool) to predominantly red (warm) bars after 1980 is stark. The 2019 anomaly of +1.52°C stands as the highest ever recorded.

Australia's 10 Hottest Years on Record

All 10 of Australia's hottest years have occurred since 2005, reflecting the accelerating pace of warming.

💡 2019 and 2023 are exceptional outliers — both exceeded the previous record by a wide margin.

Section 02 — Across the Map

Where is Australia Warming Fastest?

Warming is not uniform across the continent. While every state has experienced significant temperature rises, inland and southern regions have warmed at a faster rate than coastal areas.

Average Maximum Temperature by State (°C)

Mean annual maximum temperature across Australian states. Data source: Bureau of Meteorology ACORN-SAT, 2020–2024.

💡 The Northern Territory and Western Australia record the highest average maximum temperatures, while Tasmania remains the coolest state.

Average Annual Maximum Temperature — Capital Cities

Comparison of mean annual maximum temperature across Australia's eight capital cities. Darwin leads as Australia's hottest capital.

💡 Darwin's average maximum temperature is nearly 10°C higher than Hobart's, illustrating the dramatic climate range across Australia.

Section 03 — Season by Season

Which Seasons Are Changing Most?

Not all seasons are warming equally. Summer maximum temperatures have risen the most sharply, while winters — though still warming — show a more gradual trend across most of the continent.

Monthly Temperature Heatmap by Decade

Average maximum temperature by month and decade for Sydney. Darker red = hotter. Warming visible across all months but strongest in summer.

💡 January and February show the largest temperature increase across decades — summers are getting hotter faster than any other season.

Seasonal Temperature Trends, 1960–2024

Average seasonal maximum temperatures in Australia. Summer (Dec–Feb) shows the steepest upward trend.

💡 Summer temperatures have risen by approximately 1.2°C since 1960 — nearly double the rate of winter warming.

Section 04 — Extreme Events

Heatwaves, Drought and Record Breakers

The most tangible impact of climate change is not the gradual average warming — it is the increasing frequency of extreme heat events. Days above 40°C are becoming more common across Australia's major cities.

Days Above 40°C Per Year — Capital Cities

Average number of days per year exceeding 40°C. Data from BOM Climate Data Online, 2015–2024.

💡 Adelaide and Perth experience the most extreme heat days of any capital city — averaging over 10 days above 40°C per year.

Annual Rainfall Trend by City, 2010–2024

Annual rainfall totals for selected Australian cities. Southern cities show a declining trend while northern cities remain variable.

💡 Perth has experienced a sustained decline in annual rainfall since 2010, consistent with the long-term drying trend in southwest Western Australia.

Maximum Temperature vs Rainfall — Australian Cities

Each point represents a city-year observation. Hotter cities generally receive less rainfall, reinforcing the heat-drought relationship across Australia.

💡 The inverse relationship between temperature and rainfall is clear — as cities get hotter, rainfall tends to decrease, creating compounding risk for drought-prone regions.
